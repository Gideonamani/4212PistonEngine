# Lesson coverage audit, 12 October 2026

Each of the 18 syllabus lessons was read, step by step and check by check, against the learning outcomes the instructor wrote for it ([lesson-objectives.md](lesson-objectives.md)). This page says where the lessons teach those outcomes, where they only touch them, and where they do not teach them at all. It is the starting plan for reworking the lessons.

How to read it. **Covered**: a step teaches the outcome explicitly. **Partial**: touched on, implied, taught only in passing, or taught in another lesson. **Missing**: no step teaches it. These are one reader's judgements from reading the text, not measurements, so use them to plan and to argue with, not as a score. The order of lessons is the new one (Cooling is lesson 6 and Parts and Construction lesson 7; Valve Operating is lesson 8 and Accessories and Drives lesson 9).

## What cuts across the lessons

1. **Leading particulars are in the wrong lesson.** Lesson 12 has five outcomes about reading an engine data sheet and none of its steps teach them. Lesson 14 (Engine Requirements) teaches exactly that in steps 7 to 11 (data plate, the GTSIO-520-H specification pages, reading the numbers). Move that material to lesson 12 and let lesson 14 spend its steps on the requirements it is missing.
2. **Lessons 1 to 6 are the thinnest on checks.** Lessons 1, 2 and 6 have two checks each; lessons 9 to 18 are held to at least eight checks of three kinds, with hints (the syllabus test). The same standard should apply to lessons 1 to 8 as they are reworked.
3. **Lesson 9 reads as a set of model exercises** ("select the idler, scrub the motion") with little explanation, and its steps carry no media plan. It should explain first and use the model second, and then it can come under the same rules as lessons 10 to 18. Until then the syllabus test exempts it by name.
4. **Lesson 17's regulatory material is entirely US (FAA).** The instructor's note says it should be anchored to the Tanzanian framework (TCAA), not assumed from another jurisdiction. The principle can be taught now (category and regulatory status decide who may do what, so find out first); the Tanzanian specifics need the instructor's source.
5. **Lesson 18 prepares for Practical B only.** Practical A (the motorised cutaway engine) and Practical C (dismantle and reassemble a power assembly, tappet servicing, valve-spring checking) have outcomes but no steps. The details of the trainer and the rigs are the instructor's to supply.
6. **Lessons 8, 11, 13, 15 and 16 carry maintenance and inspection material that no outcome of their own asks for** (valve and spring inspection, exhaust inspection, compression tests, storage). It is good material and mostly belongs to lesson 16's outcomes (O11 to O14 name them). Lesson 16 can point at those lessons instead of repeating them.
7. **Facts to check as lessons are reworked.** The existing text was drafted quickly and some of it does not hold up. For example, lesson 2 calls the 1913 Renault V8 "water-cooled", but Renault's 1907 to 1913 V8 aero engines were air-cooled (the Smithsonian and the Aircraft Engine Historical Society both say so); and it lists the Pratt & Whitney Wasp and the Wright Cyclone as the radials of the era's record flights, one of which, the Spirit of St Louis, flew with a Wright J-5C Whirlwind. Every claim in a reworked lesson should be checked against a source before it stays.
8. **The reorder is cheap in the text.** The lessons barely name each other (one incidental "accessory case" mention), so moving Cooling before Parts and Valve Operating before Accessories needed no rewriting. Lesson 9 can now say the cam gear was met in lesson 8.

## Suggested order for the rework

Take the lessons in the order a learner meets them, one commit each, because every lesson's rework feeds the next (vocabulary in lesson 3 is used everywhere after it):

1. Lessons 1 and 2 (history), then 3 (terminologies).
2. Lessons 4 to 7 (cycles, classification, cooling, parts).
3. Lessons 8 to 10 (valve, accessories, power generation), taking lesson 14's data-sheet steps into lesson 12 at the same time.
4. Lessons 11 to 16, and 17 and 18 once the instructor's source material for the TCAA rules and the practical rigs is to hand.

Each reworked lesson is held to the same rules as lessons 8 to 18: every step names its source, steps stay short, at least eight checks of at least three kinds with hints, and the lesson stays marked as awaiting the instructor's review until the instructor says otherwise.

### Where the course slides disagree with the sources

The reworked lessons follow the sources. Each affected step says so in its note, so the slides can be corrected.

1. Slide 17 calls the 1913 Renault V8 water-cooled. Renault's V8 aero engines were air-cooled (Smithsonian; Aircraft Engine Historical Society).
2. Slide 45 defines the powerplant as "an aircraft engine, or the system that produces thrust". The FAA handbook glossary (PDF p. 479) defines it as the complete installation of an aircraft engine, propeller and all accessories needed for its proper function, and 14 CFR 25.901 and EASA CS 23.2400 define the powerplant installation by what its components do.
3. Slide 26 lists the Kestrel and the V-1710 among the 1,000 hp engines of the 1930s. Their outputs varied by variant; only the Merlin II's 1,030 hp in 1938 could be confirmed.
4. The notes of slide 24 name the Wasp and the Cyclone beside the Spirit of St Louis, which flew a Wright J-5C Whirlwind.

The FAA handbook also disagrees with itself on how much of the fuel's heat becomes power: about one-fourth in the cooling chapter (PDF p. 278), about 34 percent in the power chapter (PDF p. 56). Lesson 6 says "roughly a quarter to a third" and cites both pages.

### The Tanzanian rules

Lessons 16, 17 and 18 and the maintenance words in lesson 3 now cite the Tanzanian Civil Aviation (Airworthiness) Regulations, revised edition 2026, read from the TCAA website (regulations 2, 21, 22, 26, 29, 30, 31, 41 and 42; lesson 18 uses regulations 30 and 31, the manufacturer's data, tools and standard of work, and the list of what an inspector checks on the engine and nacelle). The TCAA states that its published copy is not authoritative; the Government Printer's version is. The same website lists the Approved Maintenance Organisation, Personnel Licensing and General Aviation operating regulations, used in lesson 17. No regulation for light-sport or recreational aircraft was found by title on the regulations list, and none in the airworthiness advisory circulars, general advisory circulars and rules pages read (page 1 of each list; page 2 of the two circular lists was not read). Lesson 17 therefore rests on regulation 16 of the Airworthiness Regulations (a restricted certificate for microlight, experimental amateur-built and kit-built aircraft) and says so in its note; please confirm whether TCAA has a current circular on light-sport or recreational aircraft. (The instructor has confirmed lesson 17 as written.)

### More places where the course slides disagree with the sources

5. Slide 182 (Firing Order - H.O.) gives the interval between power events as 120 degrees for four cylinders and 60 degrees for six. 720 degrees divided by the number of cylinders gives 180 and 120. Lesson 10 follows the arithmetic.
6. Slide 207 shows the spark at 22 degrees BTDC for the GTSIO-520-H. The manual's -H page says 20 degrees BTC on both magnetos; 22 degrees is the IO-520's figure (lesson 3 already follows the manual).
7. Slide 207 reads the S in GTSIO as supercharged. Lesson 5 follows the EASA key (T or TS for a turbo-supercharging system).
8. The slides say accessory drive shafts have a shear section that protects the gear train if a pump seizes. The GTSIO-520 manual pages read do not describe it; the FAA handbook shows the principle for a turbine engine's oil pump (PDF p. 269). Lesson 9 states it as a general design feature and says to check the manual.
