# Langley / Manly-Balzer engine: research before the reconstruction

Reviewed 6 October 2026. Target: a detailed educational reconstruction of the **large five-cylinder, 5 in x 5.5 in, water-cooled stationary radial engine** that Charles M. Manly built in the Smithsonian shops in 1901 (completed December 1901, tested from January 1902) for Samuel P. Langley's Aerodrome A, in the final form drawn in Plates 78-81 of the 1911 *Langley Memoir*. This is a source-led CAD study, not a certified manufacturing reproduction, and it does not establish operating dynamics. The Wright reconstruction (`cad-studies/wright-1903/revision-2/`) is the sister study; the two are meant to be compared side by side.

## What a student should see (the purpose that decides what gets researched)

1. Five cylinders spaced 72 degrees apart firing 1-3-5-2-4, one impulse every 144 degrees of crank, against the Wright's four inline cylinders.
2. **One crank pin carrying five connecting rods**: a master rod that wraps the pin, and four link rods whose bronze slippers slide on the master rod's sleeve.
3. **One double-lobed cam, turning backwards at a quarter of crank speed**, operating all five exhaust valves through five punch rods; five automatic (suction) inlet valves with no drive at all.
4. Steel-shell construction: a thin steel cylinder with a shrunk cast-iron liner and a brazed sheet-steel water jacket.

## Configuration and source decisions

The drawings (Plates 78-81) record the **design** of the engine as built; the surviving engine in the Smithsonian is a physical object whose later history (1914 Curtiss tests, exhibit changes, restoration) is not documented in the sources read. The model therefore follows the **Memoir drawings and text**, and uses the Annals' exhibit photographs only as cross-checks. It is the **large 1901 engine in its improved-cylinder, master-rod form**, not the 1900 experimental engine (Balzer's cylinders, wet cloths, pure slipper bearing, 4 3/16 in bore), and not the small engine of the quarter-size model.

Scope (decided with the user on 6 October 2026): the **engine proper** of Manly's weight table (crankshaft, connecting rods, pistons, cylinders with their valves and oil cups, port and starboard crank-chamber drums with cam, cam gears and punch rods, plugs, water pipes, inlet manifold, primary and secondary sparkers and wires, balance arms with braces), the four things the plates draw on the port and starboard bed plates (bed plates, starting mechanism and pump-drive bevel gear, sparking gear train), and, because the user asked for it, **the two flywheels and short transmission-shaft stubs**, so that the model looks like the engine on its test frame in Plates 82-84. The flywheels are *not* in the 124.17 lb figure; their masses (3,946 g starboard, 3,234 g port) are separate mass-check targets. Outside the selected assembly, each with a reason in `inventory.json`: spark coil, batteries, carburetor, inlet pipe, tanks, circulating pump and radiator, and the Aerodrome frame, transmission shafts, gears and propellers. The intended end product is an Explore card (operating-cycle clip, systems exploded view, audited and released like the Wright reconstruction).

Four decisions were needed where the sources disagree or are silent:

- **Rod weights.** The table prints 5,005 g for the connecting rods; the total requires 5,070 g. The model's mass check uses the total (56,323 g = 124.17 lb) and tolerates this 65 g ambiguity.
- **Cylinder numbering and positions.** Firing order 1-3-5-2-4 is documented; the position of cylinder 1 and the rotation direction in an engine-fixed frame are not. Numbering consecutively in the direction of crank rotation is the only assignment that makes 1-3-5-2-4 work, so the model adopts it (cylinder 1 at the top, crankshaft turning clockwise as viewed in Plate 82), labelled as a modelling choice.
- **Balzer-versus-Manly credit.** The 1933 affidavits (Hewitt, Newham) were written for Balzer's side three decades later; they are used only for construction facts that agree with the Memoir (diamond tubular bearers, two side rings carrying the main bearings, cam and followers kept from Balzer's engine).
- **Weight basis.** Where the brief and later sources quote 124 lb, 125 lb, 130 lb, 136 lb, 200 lb, 207.5 lb or 209.6 lb, each is a different inclusion boundary (Manly's own letters give 130 lb in March 1902 and 125 lb in June 1902; the table gives 124.17 lb). The model's mass check targets the table.

## Operating and construction understanding

**Load and motion.** Gas pressure acts on a cast-iron piston with a domed, two-rib crown and four narrow lap-joint rings; a hollow case-hardened steel gudgeon pin of 7/8 in outside diameter carries a rod of 7/8 in diameter (solid for the master rod, drilled with a 5/8 in hole for the four link rods). The master rod is a steel forging whose big end is a **split steel sleeve with a split bronze lining**, the two splits at right angles so they can be assembled on the crank pin; the upper half is integral with the rod and the sleeve is turned truly round everywhere but where the rod joins it. The four link rods end in **bronze shoes** that bear on the outside of that sleeve and slide on it to take up the changing rod angle, held on by **cone nuts screwed to the sleeve and locked by jam nuts**; the shoes are slightly under 60 degrees wide, leaving about one sixth of the circumference (60 degrees) free so that neighbouring shoes can shift. This works because the link rods never turn on the crank pin: they slip a short distance over the sleeve, and only the master rod bears on the rotating pin. The single hollow crankshaft has one crank arm and one hollow crank pin and runs in two bronze-bushed bearings, one in each drum; the crank carries a threaded section for cone nuts that was left on the pin from the earlier pure-slipper design.

**Cylinder assembly.** Each cylinder is a closed-end seamless steel shell with a flange screwed and brazed near the bottom, by which it bolts to the supporting drum; the combustion chamber, machined from a solid steel forging, enters the cylinder near the top at the side and is brazed on; a sheet-steel jacket 0.020 in thick is brazed over shell and chamber, ending in a ring that encircles the cylinder near mid-length. A **centrally placed spark plug** screws into the dome. The chamber carries an **automatic inlet valve** in a removable cast-iron seat held by a nut, with a coil spring, and below it a **mechanical exhaust valve** whose stem passes through a long coil spring and is struck by the punch rod; below the exhaust seat is a chamber with a side outlet, so exhaust leaves behind the aviator and does not heat the port main bearing. Plate 78 shows both valve heads, both springs, the plug and the jacket stubs in section.

**Valve train and ignition.** A small gear on the crankshaft by the crank arm drives, through gears on studs on the port drum, a **double-pointed cam turning at one quarter crank speed in the reverse direction** on the outside of the port drum's hub. Five punch rods, riding on hardened rollers on the cam, lift the exhaust stems; the exhaust cam slant was greatly reduced on 8 January 1902 because the push rods had been smashing. Ignition is jump-spark, copied from the De Dion engine: a **single primary sparker** (a cam at 2.5 times crank speed acting on a pawl on a spring, making and breaking the primary circuit five times per two revolutions), one coil, and a **secondary distributor** (a disc with a brush at half crank speed running over a five-section commutator, one section per plug). The sparking gears are driven by a gear on a sleeve that telescopes over the hub of the starboard drum and ends in a ring fixed to the crankshaft. The first commutator insulation was red fibre, which failed in damp weather (zinc oxide), and was replaced by hard rubber.

**Gas, coolant, oil.** The carburetor (a tank of tupelo-wood lumps fed with gasoline, warmed by air drawn past the cylinders; outside the selected assembly) feeds a **circular manifold of bent tube with five branches**, cut in three places and joined by flanges so it could be assembled on the engine in the frame; an auxiliary air valve is a rotating sleeve on the vertical pipe. Coolant enters a circular manifold on the **starboard** side from a **centrifugal pump driven at three times engine speed** through a vertical shaft with a splined telescoping section and bevel gears, and leaves through a circular manifold on the **port** side, through two connections, to radiating tubes. Lubrication is by hand-fed oil cups: one on the port bed plate feeds a groove in the port bushing, the hollow crankshaft, a pipe, the crank arm and the hollow crank pin, then holes through the master sleeve to the underside of the four shoes; five crescent-shaped sheet-steel cups (0.003 in) on the cylinders feed each piston through tubes; the gudgeon pins are oiled by centrifugal oil running up the rods; and a cup on the starboard bed plate feeds the starboard bearing.

**Starting and balance.** A worm wheel fastened to the port end of the crankshaft is engaged by a **sliding worm screw on a tubular starting shaft**, turned by a ratchet crank; a spring-pressed pawl holds the worm out of mesh until the operator pulls a button, and the worm climbs out of mesh when the engine fires. The bevel gear for the pump drive sits on the hub of that worm wheel. Balance is by two **balance arms** bolted between the flanges that couple the transmission shafts to the crankshaft, each ending in a lozenge-shaped lug braced by an inclined tube and plate, instead of on the crank or the flywheel rims, so as to use a larger radius for less weight. Meyer's footnotes record the cost of the conversion: run as a rotary the engine would have needed no flywheels (20 lb) and about 43 lb of cooling equipment (water, condenser, pump), at a small power loss to windage.

**Assembly order.** The sources describe fragments, not a full order: the master-sleeve halves assemble on the crank pin (hence the two splits at right angles); the cylinders bolt to drum rings on flanges drilled with a jig; the cam ring slides over the port hub with the gear train on studs on the drum; the manifolds are cut so they can be fitted in the frame. A full order is not claimed.

## Readiness decision and validation plan

The sources now support a substantially complete **teaching** reconstruction of the engine proper: its configuration, the construction of every principal part, the load, valve, ignition, coolant and oil paths, the principal documented dimensions and all of the drive ratios. They do **not** support a manufacturing drawing set, an authenticated valve-timing law or spring rates, and they do not tell us whether the surviving museum engine matches the 1901 drawings in every detail. Nothing has been checked against the physical engine.

Before generation: every line of Manly's weight table has a disposition (`inventory.json`, `expected weight-table items` in `covers`); every mechanism has its dossier rows in `operating-motion.md`; the reversed 1/4-speed cam, firing order and cylinder numbering have been checked to close against each other; and the packaging record in `operating-motion.md` lists what sits in what. After generation: all solids valid and a STEP round trip; the dimensional checks above (bore, stroke, shell, liner, jacket, rod and pin diameters); a **mass check against the weight table within a stated tolerance** (the table gives eleven independent masses, which makes this a real test of shape); rest-pose and between-key interference audits of the exported file under the project's rules; the motion ratios on the exported GLB. Resemblance to the plates is the weakest rung and is reported separately: overlays of the model's section and end view on Plates 78 and 79, with the residuals. Coverage, geometry, mass, mechanism and source agreement are reported as separate categories, and the research gate passing is not a certificate of authenticity.
